Self-play against only the latest opponent cycles and forgets. We keep every attacker and defender, train each new one from the base model against the Nash mixture of the other side, and get the safest defender among the self-play methods we compare.

Key observation · pool@k

Six attackers together break 3.4× as many prompts as one

pool@k is the fraction of held-out prompts broken by at least one of k independently trained attackers, each queried once. It is pass@k taken across models instead of samples.

We trained six copies of one attacker recipe, differing only in which training samples they saw. Their success rates span 10×, and they fail in different places: the two strongest break 81 prompts each but share only 38.

So pool@k keeps rising with k and has not flattened at six. Each run settles on a different subset of strategies. A defender trained against one attacker sees one subset; PSRO trains it against the whole population.

Motivation

Why a single policy is not enough

One policy cannot hold the coverage

Strategies collapse while tokens look more diverse

We label each attack by its opening framing and track the entropy over those strategies while one attacker trains. It falls to 0.02× its starting value. Token-level entropy, the usual diversity monitor, rises to 1.56× over the same steps.

The attacker writes longer, more varied sentences around fewer ideas, so a token monitor reports the opposite of what is happening. Training longer does not fix this, because RL concentrates on whatever strategy pays most right now.

Naive self-play cycles

Each generation trades safety for helpfulness, or back

Naive self-play trains each defender against the newest attacker only. Over five generations it swings between refusing broadly, which is safe but unhelpful, and complying broadly, which is helpful but unsafe. The average of the two stays between 0.76 and 0.82.

Defenses are also forgotten: on WildJailbreak the fourth defender is broken by 10% of prompts, the fifth by 74%.

Method

PSRO, adapted to language models

  1. Keep every trained attacker and defender frozen in the population.
  2. Measure each new member against all members of the other side, which adds one row or column to the payoff matrix.
  3. Solve the matrix for Nash mixtures over each population.
  4. Train the next policy with REINFORCE on a fresh LoRA from the base model, against opponents sampled from the Nash mixture. Roles alternate.

The matrix grows quadratically and every cell needs thousands of judged conversations. A racing evaluation budget and a rollout cache cut that cost by about an order of magnitude.

Findings

What we found

Safer than other self-play methods

Starting from a model with its safety training removed, ours ends up refusing harmful requests most reliably.

It keeps improving; the others don't

Each round of ours is better overall. Training only against the newest opponent goes back and forth, and weighting all opponents equally goes nowhere.

Old opponents still matter

A newer attacker is not always the strongest. When training the final defender, our method picked older attackers most often and skipped the newest one.

Every new member starts from scratch

If we keep training the previous model instead, it breaks within two rounds and starts giving blank answers.

Strong attackers game the scoring

Once the attackers got good, they started earning points by rewriting harmless requests into harmful ones. We had to fix the scoring rules.

Keeping score is cheap

Testing a new member against everyone takes a small fraction of the time it takes to train it.
