Self-play against only the latest opponent cycles and forgets. We keep every attacker and defender, train each new one from the base model against the Nash mixture of the other side, and get the safest defender among the self-play methods we compare.

Key observation · pool@k

Six attackers together break 3.4× as many prompts as one

pool@k is the fraction of held-out prompts broken by at least one of k independently trained attackers, each queried once. It is pass@k taken across models instead of samples.

We trained six copies of one attacker recipe, differing only in which training samples they saw. Their success rates span 10×, and they fail in different places: the two strongest break 81 prompts each but share only 38.

So pool@k keeps rising with k and has not flattened at six. Each run settles on a different subset of strategies. A defender trained against one attacker sees one subset; PSRO trains it against the whole population.

Motivation

Why a single policy is not enough

One policy cannot hold the coverage

Strategies collapse while tokens look more diverse

We label each attack by its opening framing and track the entropy over those strategies while one attacker trains. It falls to 0.02× its starting value. Token-level entropy, the usual diversity monitor, rises to 1.56× over the same steps.

The attacker writes longer, more varied sentences around fewer ideas, so a token monitor reports the opposite of what is happening. Training longer does not fix this, because RL concentrates on whatever strategy pays most right now.

Naive self-play cycles

Each generation trades safety for helpfulness, or back

Naive self-play trains each defender against the newest attacker only. Over five generations it swings between refusing broadly, which is safe but unhelpful, and complying broadly, which is helpful but unsafe. The average of the two stays between 0.76 and 0.82.

Defenses are also forgotten: on WildJailbreak the fourth defender is broken by 10% of prompts, the fifth by 74%.

Method

PSRO, adapted to language models

  1. Keep every trained attacker and defender frozen in the population.
  2. Measure each new member against all members of the other side, which adds one row or column to the payoff matrix.
  3. Solve the matrix for Nash mixtures over each population.
  4. Train the next policy with REINFORCE on a fresh LoRA from the base model, against opponents sampled from the Nash mixture. Roles alternate.

The matrix grows quadratically and every cell needs thousands of judged conversations. A racing evaluation budget and a rollout cache cut that cost by about an order of magnitude.

Results

Safer than every other self-play method

All Llama rows start from the abliterated Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, whose safety training has been removed. Safety averages seven harmful-prompt measures; helpfulness averages benign compliance on WildJailbreak and XSTest. PSRO pays a moderate helpfulness cost.

Findings

What the experiments show

RQ1

Does population-based self-play beat prior self-play?

The safest self-play defender

PSRO reaches 0.955 safety. Fictitious self-play reaches 0.890; Self-RedTeam and naive self-play at most 0.81.

Biggest gain on the hardest jailbreaks

On Do-Anything-Now prompts, 40–63% of attacks get through single-policy methods, but only 5–13% get through the population methods.

Nash weighting is safer than equal weighting

Same kind of population, different weights: PSRO refuses more unsafe prompts than fictitious self-play on XSTest (0.880 vs 0.778) and StrongREJECT (0.983 vs 0.892).

Not an artifact of the training judge

Three judges never used in training all rate PSRO the least harmful. It is not hiding behind empty replies: Gemini flags only 18 of 250 benign answers as non-answers.

Works on a second model family

On an abliterated Qwen3-8B, PSRO raises safety from 0.429 to 0.983.

The helpfulness cost is narrow

Lost helpfulness is on harmless prompts written to look adversarial (0.932 → 0.678). Ordinary safe prompts are answered as before (0.944), and reasoning stays within 0.044 of the base model.

RQ2

Does PSRO improve as the population grows?

Each new defender is harder to beat

Even attackers trained later get less out of each new Nash defender: the best attack payoff falls at every iteration, from −0.28 to −0.70.

Safety holds while helpfulness recovers

Safety stays between 0.87 and 0.97 in every generation, while helpfulness climbs from 0.74 back to 0.84–0.88 from the third generation on.

The baselines do not improve

Fictitious self-play ends where it started, and naive self-play's fifth defender falls back to the level of its first.

RQ3

How far can the current reward carry the population?

Strong attackers find bugs in the reward

In an earlier run the attacker learned to turn harmless requests into harmful ones to collect reward: from 0.7% of rewrites at the start to 41.7% by generation 5.

Then attacks collapse onto templates

It also swapped hard requests for easy ones it already knew, until 95% of its attacks opened with the same clause.

A patched reward fixes most of it

The patch stops the label rewriting. Request swapping remains in 10% of the final attacker's rewrites; it inflates the attacker's score, but the defender still learns to refuse.

RQ4

Why does keeping a population help?

One policy narrows to a few strategies

A single attacker goes from 191 distinct strategies to 4, while its token entropy rises 1.56×. A token-level diversity monitor would miss the collapse.

New members must start fresh

Continuing the previous defender collapses by generation 2, with 96–100% of answers empty. Starting each member from the base model keeps every generation trainable.

No generation beats all the others

The newer D3 does worse against A3 than the older D2 does. So the equilibrium mixes two or three past defenders, until one (D5) beats every attacker.

RQ5

Is the payoff matrix worth its cost?

The payoff matrix is cheap

Adding a new member's row or column takes about 13 minutes. Training that member takes 3.8 hours.

Equal weighting wastes training

Up to about 80% of opponent draws go to opponents the equilibrium ignores. Fictitious self-play's defenders run all 300 steps; PSRO's stop after 172 on average (1,840 vs 2,960 steps in total).

Evaluation is about 10× cheaper

Racing stops sampling a cell once its value is settled (1,336 instead of 4,000 episodes per cell, same accuracy), and a cache reuses every matchup.
