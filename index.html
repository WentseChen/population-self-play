TL;DR Independently trained attackers find different jailbreaks, so a population is a stronger opponent than any single attacker. We train one defender against the whole population, and it is the safest among the self-play methods we compare.

Abstract

While self-play promises an unbounded supply of synthetic data for LLM post-training, standard single-policy methods quickly plateau. In attacker–defender self-play for safety training, we find that independently training the same attacker recipe produces diverse strategies. We measure this with pool@k, the fraction of prompts broken by at least one of k attackers: it reaches 3.4× the single-run value at k = 6 and continues to rise. This makes a population a stronger opponent than any individual attacker. We build on this finding with population-based self-play via Policy-Space Response Oracles: each new attacker or defender starts from the base model and trains against the Nash mixture of the entire opposing population. A single defender can thus learn to resist the population’s strategies while requiring only one model run at inference. To scale population payoff matrix evaluation at 8B parameters, we introduce a racing evaluation budget and a rollout cache. On standard safety benchmarks, our defender achieves 95.5% average safety, outperforming baseline self-play methods (at most 89.0%) at a moderate helpfulness cost.

Why train against a population

In jailbreak self-play, an attacker writes prompts and a defender must answer them safely. There are endless ways to attack, and no training set or test covers more than a small part of them. So any single training run settles on whatever worked in the part it happened to see: a valid answer, but only one of many.

Every training run is a draw

We trained the same attacker six times. The recipe was the same; the runs differ only in which sample of the training corpus they consume. Each run still found different winning strategies: the two strongest break 81 of 200 prompts each, yet share only 38. Training once is like drawing one ticket. You get one of many valid outcomes, and which one is luck.

pool@k asks what you win by holding k tickets: the fraction of prompts broken by at least one of k independently trained attackers, each tried once. It is like pass@k, but across training runs instead of samples.

We could run all six, but nobody wants to run six models on every request. We want one model that has this coverage, so we make the population its opponent.

A single policy narrows, then forgets

Why not simply train one model for longer? Because reinforcement learning piles onto whichever strategy pays most. An attacker ends up with only a few strategies, even though its wording looks more and more varied (left). And training against only the newest opponent makes the defender forget: it slides back and forth between refusing everything and answering everything, while PSRO stays on the better side (right).

Method

We build on PSRO (Lanctot et al., 2017) and its use for red-teaming (GRTS, Ma et al.). It is a loop of four steps:

  1. Keep everyone. Every trained attacker and defender stays in the population, frozen.
  2. Score every matchup. Each new member plays against everyone on the other side. This adds one row or column to the score table.
  3. Find who still matters. From the table we work out the mix of opponents that is hardest to beat (the Nash equilibrium).
  4. Train the next member from the original model against that mix. Attackers and defenders take turns.

The table grows quickly, so we stop testing a matchup once its result is clear, and we never replay one we have already played.

Findings

Training only against the latest opponent goes in circles

Attack and defense strategies beat each other in cycles, like rock-paper-scissors. A model that only chases the newest opponent keeps trading one weakness for another.

Newer is not always stronger

A newer defender can lose to an attack that an older one stopped. So the best practice mix often leans on older opponents. For the fifth defender, the two newest attackers get no weight at all.

Beating past opponents prepares you for future ones

A defender trained against a well-chosen mix of past attackers also holds up against attackers trained after it. Even the strongest of them does worse every round.

Choosing opponents well beats training longer

Scoring a new member against everyone takes minutes; training it takes hours. Weighting all opponents equally skips the scoring, but wastes practice on opponents that are already beaten. It trains longer and still ends up less safe.

Members need room to learn

When we kept training the previous model instead of starting fresh, it fell behind in the second round and then broke down into empty answers. The likely reason is that a model already trained hard has too little left to explore.

A strong opponent is the best bug finder for your reward

In an earlier run, once attackers got good, they found a loophole. Instead of rewording the request they were given, they swapped in a harmful one, because the scoring paid for that. The population exposed a flaw we had not noticed.

Eventually the reward, not the algorithm, is the limit

After we fixed that loophole, a milder trick remained: swapping a hard request for an easier one. As self-play gets stronger, better scoring becomes the main thing to improve.

Check with judges the model never trained against

A model can learn to please the judge that trained it. We re-scored its answers with three outside judges, and ours gives the fewest harmful answers under every one.

The biggest gains come on the most creative attacks

Role-play jailbreaks ask the model to become a character with no rules. They beat models trained against one opponent. A population has seen enough variety to block almost all of them.

Not tied to one model family

The same recipe makes both Llama and Qwen far safer, each starting from a version with its safety training removed.
