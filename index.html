Motivation

Why train against a population

In jailbreak self-play, an attacker writes prompts and a defender must answer safely. The space of possible attacks is open-ended, and no training set, benchmark or judge covers more than a small part of it. So any single training run settles on a strategy that fits the part it happened to see: a valid solution, but only one of many.

A training run is a draw

Every training run is a lottery ticket

We trained the same attacker six times. Same recipe, same data; only the order of training samples changed. Each run still ended up with different winning strategies: the two strongest break 81 prompts each, yet share only 38. Training once is like drawing one ticket: you get one of many valid outcomes, and which one is luck. We call this partial convergence.

Problem 1 · A single policy collapses

An attacker's strategies collapse while its wording looks more diverse

One might expect a policy trained for longer to pick up all of these strategies by itself. Reinforcement learning works against this: it piles probability onto whichever strategy pays most right now. We trained one attacker against a fixed defender. As it trains, the entropy over the attack strategies it uses falls to 0.02× its starting value.

Token-level entropy, the usual diversity monitor, rises to 1.56× over the same steps. The attacker writes longer, more varied sentences around fewer ideas, so the monitor reports the opposite of what is happening. Training longer does not fix this.

Problem 2 · Naive self-play forgets

It does not keep what it learned

Naive self-play trains each new defender against the newest attacker only, so nothing asks it to keep resisting earlier attacks. On WildJailbreak, the fourth defender is broken by 10% of prompts; the fifth, trained against a newer attacker, by 74%.

Strategies beat each other in cycles, so the defender is pushed around a loop instead of up a ladder: it swings between refusing broadly, which is safe but unhelpful, and complying broadly, which is helpful but unsafe.

Naive self-play defenders D1–D5
Can a single policy, trained against an equilibrium-weighted population, capture that population's coverage? And what does it take to make this work for LLM self-play?

Method

PSRO, adapted to language models

  1. Keep every trained attacker and defender frozen in the population.
  2. Measure each new member against all members of the other side, which adds one row or column to the payoff matrix.
  3. Solve the matrix for Nash mixtures over each population.
  4. Train the next policy with REINFORCE on a fresh LoRA from the base model, against opponents sampled from the Nash mixture. Roles alternate.

The matrix grows quadratically and every cell needs thousands of judged conversations. A racing evaluation budget and a rollout cache cut that cost by about an order of magnitude.

Findings

Training only against the latest opponent goes in circles

Attack and defense strategies beat each other in cycles, like rock-paper-scissors. A model that only chases the newest opponent keeps trading one weakness for another instead of making progress.

Newer is not always stronger

A newer defender can lose to an attack an older one stopped. So the best practice mix often leans on older opponents. Here the newest attacker was not picked at all.

Beating past opponents prepares you for future ones

A defender trained against a well-chosen mix of past attackers also holds up against attackers trained after it. The strongest of them does worse every round.

Choosing opponents well beats training longer

Measuring how a new member does against everyone takes minutes; training it takes hours. Weighting all opponents equally skips that measurement, wastes practice on opponents already beaten, trains longer, and still ends up less safe.

Each new member should start from scratch

A model that has already been trained hard has little room left to learn. Continuing it falls behind and then breaks into blank answers. Starting each member from the original model keeps every round trainable.

A strong opponent is the best bug finder for your reward

Once attackers got good, they found a loophole: instead of rewording the request they were given, they swapped in a harmful one, because the scoring paid for that. The population exposed a flaw we had not noticed.

Eventually the reward, not the algorithm, is the limit

After we fixed that loophole, a milder trick remained: swapping a hard request for an easier one. As self-play gets stronger, better scoring becomes the main thing to improve.

Check with judges the model never trained against

A model can learn to please the judge that trained it. Re-scoring with three outside judges shows the safety is real: ours gives the fewest harmful answers under every one.

The result: the safest self-play defender

Starting from a model whose safety training was removed, keeping and weighting the whole population ends up refusing harmful requests most reliably.

The biggest gains come on the most creative attacks

Role-play jailbreaks, which ask the model to become a character with no rules, beat models trained against one opponent. A population has seen enough variety to block almost all of them.

Safe does not have to mean unhelpful

Ours still answers everyday questions as often as the original model. The cost is narrower: it turns down more harmless questions that are worded to look suspicious.

Not tied to one model family

The same recipe makes both Llama and Qwen far safer, each starting from a version with its safety training removed.
