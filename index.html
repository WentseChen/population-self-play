TL;DR Independently trained attackers find different jailbreaks, so a population is a stronger opponent than any single attacker. We train one defender against the whole population, and it is the safest among the self-play methods we compare.

Abstract

While self-play promises an unbounded supply of synthetic data for LLM post-training, standard single-policy methods quickly plateau. In attacker–defender self-play for safety training, we find that independently training the same attacker recipe produces diverse strategies. We measure this with pool@k, the fraction of prompts broken by at least one of k attackers: it reaches 3.4× the single-run value at k = 6 and continues to rise. This makes a population a stronger opponent than any individual attacker. We build on this finding with population-based self-play via Policy-Space Response Oracles: each new attacker or defender starts from the base model and trains against the Nash mixture of the entire opposing population. A single defender can thus learn to resist the population’s strategies while requiring only one model run at inference. To scale population payoff matrix evaluation at 8B parameters, we introduce a racing evaluation budget and a rollout cache. On standard safety benchmarks, our defender achieves 95.5% average safety, outperforming baseline self-play methods (at most 89.0%) at a moderate helpfulness cost.

1Why train against a population

In jailbreak self-play, an attacker writes prompts and a defender must answer them safely. The space of attacks is open-ended, and no corpus, benchmark or judge covers more than a small part of it. Any single run therefore settles on a strategy that fits the part it happened to see: a valid solution, but only one of many.

1.1Every training run is a draw

We train six copies of one attacker recipe that differ only in which sample of the training corpus they consume. Their success rates span an order of magnitude, and they succeed on different prompts: the two strongest break 81 of 200 held-out prompts each, yet share only 38. Training once is like drawing one ticket. We call this partial convergence, and measure what k draws cover together:

pool@k=ES:|S|=k[Prx(some attacker in S breaks prompt x)]

This is pass@k taken across training runs instead of samples. At k = 6 it reaches 3.4 times its single-run value and is still rising.

Deploying k models would capture this coverage but costs k runs per request. We want one policy that has it, and so we make the population the opponent.

1.2A single policy narrows, then forgets

Training one policy for longer does not collect these strategies. Reinforcement learning concentrates on whichever strategy pays most, so an attacker's strategies collapse while its wording becomes more varied, and a token-level diversity monitor reads the opposite of what is happening (Figure 3a). Retraining against only the newest opponent does not help either. Nothing asks the defender to keep resisting earlier attacks, and it slides between refusing broadly and complying broadly (Figure 3b).

Can a single policy trained against an equilibrium-weighted population capture that population's coverage, and what does it take to make this work for LLM self-play?

2Method

We build on Policy-Space Response Oracles (PSRO) [1] and its use for red-teaming language models in GRTS [2]. Keep every trained attacker Ai and defender Dj frozen, and let Mij = u(Ai, Dj) be the attacker's measured payoff; the defender receives its negative. Each iteration solves this zero-sum game for a mixture over each side,

p∈arg maxpminj(p⊤M)j,q∈arg minqmaxi(Mq)i,

and trains one new member against the other side's mixture:

Anew≈arg maxAEj∼q[u(A,Dj)],Dnew≈arg minDEi∼p[u(Ai,D)].

Every new member is a fresh LoRA adapter on the base model, trained with REINFORCE. The matrix grows quadratically and each cell needs thousands of judged conversations, so we spend fewer episodes on matchups whose value is already clear (a racing budget) and never replay a finished one (a rollout cache).

3Experiments

All Llama runs start from an abliterated Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, whose refusal behaviour has been largely removed. Safety averages seven measures on harmful prompts; Helpful averages compliance on two sets of benign prompts. Naive self-play trains against the latest opponent only, and fictitious self-play [3] keeps a population but weights it uniformly. Both, and PSRO, are reported at their fifth generation.

3.1The safest defender among the self-play methods we compare

The two population methods are clearly safer than the single-policy ones, and the gap is widest on role-play jailbreaks (Do Anything Now). Between the two, PSRO is safer and fictitious self-play slightly more helpful.

The judge that scores training could itself be fooled, so we re-score the same answers with three judges never used in training. PSRO has the lowest harmful-response rate under every one (Figure 5a). The recipe also raises the safety of an abliterated Qwen3-8B (Figure 5b).

3.2The defender improves as the population grows

On held-out benchmarks, safety plus helpfulness rises over PSRO's generations, while fictitious self-play ends where it started and naive self-play goes back and forth (Figure 6a). Inside the game, the best payoff that any attacker of the final population achieves against PSRO's defender mixture falls at every iteration, including attackers trained later (Figure 6b).

3.3Newer is not always stronger, so the choice of opponent matters

Strategies beat one another in cycles. The third defender does worse against A3 than the older second defender does, so the equilibrium spreads its weight over older members and can give the newest ones none (Figure 7a). Finding these weights is cheap: filling in a new member's row or column takes minutes, while training the member takes hours. Weighting all opponents equally skips that step but spends practice on opponents that are already beaten. It trains for more steps and ends less safe (Figure 7b, Table 1).

3.4Members need room to learn

PSRO starts every member from the base model. In a warm-start variant that continues the previous member instead, the second defender fell behind and then collapsed into empty answers (Figure 8). The likely cause is that reinforcement learning had already concentrated the first defender on a narrow set of behaviours, leaving its successor too little to explore.

3.5Strong attackers exploit the reward

In an earlier run, once the attackers became strong, they found two gaps in the reward. In label drift, an attacker given a harmless request rewrote it into a harmful one to collect the reward for harmful compliance. In topic substitution, it replaced a hard harmful request with an easier one. We revised the reward before the reported run. Label drift is addressed, but substitution is not fully prevented (Figure 9b). It does not mislead the defender, which still learns to refuse these prompts, but it inflates the attacker's payoff. As self-play gets stronger, the reward becomes the part that needs work [7].

4Limitations

Every self-play method trains both an attacker and a defender over many iterations, and so costs more than a defence that trains one model once. Topic substitution is not fully prevented in the reported run.

5Related work

GRTS [2] first cast red-teaming as a two-player game solved with PSRO, and we adopt its formulation. We ask which part does the work, by comparing latest-opponent, uniformly weighted and equilibrium-weighted self-play, at 8B and on external benchmarks. Most recent methods, including Self-RedTeam [6], MAGIC [5] and MART [8], train against the opponent's current version and do not keep past opponents. Pipeline PSRO [9] and related methods scale PSRO in settings where matchups are cheap to evaluate.
